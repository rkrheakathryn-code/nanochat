# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health Program Implementation: Experiences of School Nurses Assigned in Public Schools in SGA BARMM

---

## Introduction

Adolescent sexual and reproductive health (SRH) remains a critical public health concern globally, with significant implications for the physical, psychological, and social well-being of young people. The World Health Organization (WHO, 2020) emphasizes that adolescents aged 10-19 years constitute approximately 16% of the world's population, and their sexual and reproductive health needs require comprehensive, age-appropriate interventions. School-based health programs have emerged as vital platforms for delivering SRH services, with school nurses playing a pivotal role in implementation (Denford et al., 2017). This review examines the current literature on school nurses' experiences in implementing SRH programs, focusing on the extent of implementation, challenges faced, nurse-student interactions, strategies employed, and professional insights.

---

## 1. Extent of Implementation of School-Based Sexual and Reproductive Health Programs

### 1.1 Global Perspectives on SRH Program Implementation

The implementation of school-based SRH programs varies considerably across different contexts and settings. According to UNESCO (2018), comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) programs have been adopted in over 80 countries worldwide, yet the extent and quality of implementation remain inconsistent. Fonner et al. (2014) conducted a systematic review revealing that school-based SRH interventions demonstrate significant positive effects on knowledge, attitudes, and behavioral outcomes when implemented comprehensively. However, the study also highlighted substantial gaps between policy adoption and actual implementation at the school level.

In developed countries, school nurses typically implement SRH programs through structured curricula, individual counseling, and referral services. A study by Maughan et al. (2018) in the United States found that 78% of school nurses reported providing some form of SRH education, though the comprehensiveness varied significantly based on state policies, school district guidelines, and available resources. Similarly, research in the United Kingdom by Westwood and Mullan (2017) demonstrated that school nurses play a central role in delivering sexual health services, including contraceptive advice, pregnancy testing, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) screening.

### 1.2 SRH Program Implementation in Southeast Asian Contexts

In Southeast Asian countries, including the Philippines, the implementation of school-based SRH programs faces unique cultural, religious, and political considerations. Marquez and Galban (2020) examined the implementation of the Philippine Comprehensive Sexuality Education program and found that while national policies support SRH education, actual implementation at the school level remains limited and fragmented. The study revealed that only 42% of public schools fully implemented the mandated SRH curriculum, with significant variations across regions.

Research by Haberland and Rogow (2015) emphasizes that effective SRH program implementation requires not only curriculum delivery but also the creation of supportive environments where adolescents can access confidential services. School nurses are uniquely positioned to provide this continuum of care, yet their capacity to do so depends on multiple factors including training, administrative support, and community acceptance.

### 1.3 Components of Comprehensive SRH Implementation

The literature identifies several key components that constitute comprehensive SRH program implementation. According to the International Technical Guidance on Sexuality Education (UNESCO, 2018), these include: (1) age-appropriate information on human development, relationships, and personal skills; (2) access to contraceptive information and services; (3) STI prevention and testing; (4) pregnancy prevention and support; (5) gender-based violence prevention; and (6) promotion of healthy relationships and consent.

Baltag et al. (2017) conducted a multi-country study examining school health services in Europe and found that the extent of SRH implementation by school nurses ranged from minimal (basic health education only) to comprehensive (including clinical services, counseling, and community linkages). The study identified that comprehensive implementation was associated with better health outcomes, including reduced rates of teenage pregnancy and STIs.

### 1.4 Measurement and Assessment of Implementation Fidelity

Assessing the extent of SRH program implementation requires consideration of implementation fidelity—the degree to which programs are delivered as intended. Durlak and DuPre (2008) propose that implementation fidelity encompasses five dimensions: adherence (whether program components are delivered as designed), dose (how much of the program is delivered), quality of delivery (how well components are delivered), participant responsiveness (degree of engagement), and program differentiation (whether the program differs from comparison conditions).

Research by Michielsen et al. (2010) in sub-Saharan Africa revealed that even when SRH programs are officially adopted, implementation fidelity is often compromised by inadequate training, insufficient time allocation, and lack of monitoring mechanisms. This finding resonates with studies in other contexts, suggesting that measuring the extent of implementation requires going beyond policy analysis to examine actual practices and service delivery.

## 2. Challenges and Barriers in Delivering Sexual and Reproductive Health Services

### 2.1 Organizational and Systemic Barriers

School nurses face numerous organizational challenges in implementing SRH programs. A systematic review by Mason-Jones et al. (2016) identified inadequate resources, including limited time, insufficient funding, and lack of appropriate materials, as primary barriers to effective SRH service delivery. School nurses often manage multiple health responsibilities beyond SRH, leading to role overload and competing priorities (Willgerodt, 2018).

Administrative barriers also significantly impact SRH implementation. Research by Breuner et al. (2016) found that restrictive school policies, lack of administrative support, and unclear guidelines regarding the scope of practice create substantial obstacles for school nurses. In many settings, school nurses must navigate complex approval processes to implement even basic SRH education, leading to delays and reduced program effectiveness.

### 2.2 Cultural and Religious Barriers

Cultural and religious sensitivities represent significant challenges in SRH program implementation, particularly in conservative communities. A study by Pound et al. (2017) examining barriers to school-based sex education found that cultural taboos surrounding sexuality, parental opposition based on religious beliefs, and community resistance were major impediments. School nurses often find themselves caught between professional obligations to provide evidence-based health information and community expectations that may conflict with comprehensive SRH education.

In the Philippine context, research by Upadhyay et al. (2014) highlighted the influence of Catholic Church teachings on SRH policy and implementation. The study found that school nurses frequently self-censor information about contraception and family planning to avoid controversy, thereby limiting the comprehensiveness of services provided. This phenomenon of "implementation dilution" due to cultural pressures has been documented across multiple contexts (Chandra-Mouli et al., 2015).

### 2.3 Knowledge and Training Gaps

Insufficient training and knowledge gaps among school nurses constitute another significant barrier. A study by Rushton et al. (2020) found that while school nurses generally possess basic health knowledge, many lack specialized training in adolescent sexual health, counseling techniques, and culturally sensitive communication. The study revealed that only 34% of school nurses surveyed had received formal training in comprehensive sexuality education within the past five years.

Research by Ketting et al. (2016) emphasizes that effective SRH service delivery requires not only biomedical knowledge but also skills in adolescent development, communication, and ethical decision-making. Many school nurses report feeling unprepared to address complex issues such as sexual orientation, gender identity, sexual abuse, and relationship violence (Goldfarb & Lieberman, 2021). This lack of preparedness can lead to avoidance of sensitive topics or provision of incomplete information.

### 2.4 Confidentiality and Trust Issues

Maintaining confidentiality while working within school systems presents unique challenges. Adolescents are more likely to seek SRH services when they trust that their privacy will be protected (Tylee et al., 2007). However, school nurses must balance confidentiality with mandatory reporting requirements, parental notification policies, and the communal nature of school environments where privacy is difficult to ensure.

A qualitative study by Borawski et al. (2015) found that school nurses identified confidentiality concerns as a major barrier to effective SRH service delivery. Students often fear that seeking SRH information or services will result in disclosure to parents, teachers, or peers, leading to underutilization of available services. Additionally, physical constraints such as lack of private consultation spaces further compromise confidentiality (Nystrom et al., 2018).

### 2.5 Parental and Community Opposition

Parental opposition to school-based SRH programs represents a persistent challenge. Research by Ito et al. (2006) found that parental concerns about age-appropriateness, moral implications, and perceived encouragement of sexual activity create significant barriers to program implementation. School nurses often face direct confrontation from parents who object to their children receiving SRH information or services.

Community-level opposition can also manifest through political pressure on school administrators, media campaigns against SRH programs, and attempts to restrict or eliminate services. A study by Santelli et al. (2017) documented how political and ideological opposition to comprehensive sexuality education in the United States led to the promotion of abstinence-only programs despite evidence of their ineffectiveness. School nurses working in such environments face professional and ethical dilemmas regarding their responsibility to provide evidence-based care.

### 2.6 Resource Constraints and Infrastructure Limitations

Material and infrastructural limitations significantly impede SRH program implementation. Research by Ambresin et al. (2013) identified lack of educational materials, absence of private consultation spaces, limited access to contraceptives and testing supplies, and inadequate referral networks as common barriers. In resource-constrained settings, school nurses may lack even basic supplies such as anatomical models, educational pamphlets, or condoms for demonstration purposes.

Technology gaps also present challenges, particularly in rural or underserved areas. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of digital platforms for health education and service delivery, yet many schools lack the infrastructure to support telehealth or online SRH education (Lindberg et al., 2020). School nurses in areas with limited internet connectivity or technological resources face additional barriers in reaching students with SRH information.

## 3. School Nurse-Student Interactions in Sexual and Reproductive Health Implementation

### 3.1 The Therapeutic Relationship in School Settings

The quality of nurse-student interactions fundamentally shapes the effectiveness of SRH program implementation. According to Peplau's Theory of Interpersonal Relations (1991), therapeutic relationships develop through phases of orientation, identification, exploitation, and resolution. In the school context, school nurses must establish trust and rapport with adolescent students who may feel vulnerable discussing sensitive sexual health topics.

Research by Clausson and Berg (2008) examining school nurse-student interactions found that students valued nurses who demonstrated genuine interest, non-judgmental attitudes, and respect for their autonomy. The study revealed that positive interactions were characterized by active listening, empathetic responses, and collaborative problem-solving. Conversely, interactions perceived as judgmental, dismissive, or authoritarian led to student disengagement and reluctance to seek future services.

### 3.2 Communication Strategies and Approaches

Effective communication is central to successful SRH interactions. A study by Shoveller et al. (2004) identified several communication strategies that school nurses employ when discussing sexual health with adolescents, including: using age-appropriate language, normalizing questions and concerns, providing factual information without moral judgment, and employing motivational interviewing techniques to support behavior change.

Research by Hadley et al. (2009) emphasizes the importance of cultural competence in nurse-student interactions. School nurses working with diverse student populations must navigate varying cultural norms regarding sexuality, gender roles, and family involvement in health decisions. The study found that culturally competent communication—characterized by awareness of cultural differences, respect for diverse values, and adaptation of approaches to individual contexts—significantly enhanced the quality of SRH interactions.

### 3.3 Building Trust and Ensuring Confidentiality

Trust is the foundation of effective nurse-student interactions in SRH contexts. Ford et al. (2004) conducted a qualitative study exploring adolescents' perspectives on school health services and found that confidentiality assurances were the most critical factor in determining whether students would seek SRH information or services. Students reported that explicit discussions about confidentiality limits, conducted in a reassuring manner, actually increased rather than decreased their trust in school nurses.

Research by Tylee et al. (2007) highlights that school nurses must balance confidentiality with safety concerns, particularly when students disclose sexual abuse, exploitation, or high-risk behaviors. The study found that transparent communication about mandatory reporting requirements, framed within a context of care and protection, helped maintain trust even when confidentiality could not be absolute. School nurses who clearly explained the circumstances under which information would be shared, and who involved students in decision-making processes when possible, maintained stronger therapeutic relationships.

### 3.4 Addressing Sensitive Topics and Difficult Conversations

School nurses regularly navigate challenging conversations about topics such as sexual activity, contraception, STIs, pregnancy, sexual orientation, and sexual violence. A phenomenological study by Bergh et al. (2015) explored school nurses' experiences of discussing sexuality with adolescents and found that nurses employed various strategies to manage discomfort and facilitate open dialogue. These included using educational materials as conversation starters, asking open-ended questions, validating students' feelings, and maintaining professional composure even when discussing uncomfortable topics.

Research by Eisenberg et al. (2010) found that adolescents appreciated when school nurses initiated conversations about sexual health rather than waiting for students to raise concerns. Proactive approaches, such as offering routine SRH screenings or including sexual health questions in general health assessments, normalized these topics and reduced stigma. However, the study also noted that such approaches required careful attention to timing, privacy, and individual readiness to engage.

### 3.5 Developmental Considerations in Interactions

Adolescence encompasses significant developmental changes that influence how school nurses interact with students regarding SRH. According to Erikson's theory of psychosocial development (1968), adolescents are navigating identity formation, including sexual identity, which makes them particularly sensitive to judgment and peer influence. School nurses must adapt their interaction styles to accommodate developmental stages, recognizing that early adolescents (10-13 years) have different needs and capacities than middle (14-16 years) or late adolescents (17-19 years).

A study by Sieving et al. (2017) examined developmentally appropriate approaches to adolescent SRH services and found that effective school nurses tailored their communication style, information depth, and decision-making support based on individual developmental levels rather than chronological age alone. The research emphasized the importance of assessing cognitive development, emotional maturity, and social context when determining how to structure SRH interactions.

### 3.6 Gender and Cultural Sensitivity in Interactions

Gender dynamics significantly influence nurse-student interactions in SRH contexts. Research by Marcell et al. (2017) found that male adolescents often feel marginalized in SRH programs that focus predominantly on pregnancy prevention and female reproductive health. The study revealed that school nurses who actively engaged male students in discussions about masculinity, consent, healthy relationships, and male reproductive health fostered more inclusive and effective SRH programs.

Cultural sensitivity in interactions extends beyond general cultural awareness to specific understanding of how culture shapes sexual health beliefs and behaviors. A study by Villarruel et al. (2006) examining culturally appropriate SRH interventions for Latino adolescents found that school nurses who incorporated cultural values such as familismo (family orientation) and respeto (respect) into their interactions achieved better engagement and outcomes. The research emphasized that cultural sensitivity requires ongoing learning and adaptation rather than stereotypical assumptions about cultural groups.

## 4. Strategies for Effective Implementation Despite Obstacles

### 4.1 Building Supportive Partnerships and Collaborations

School nurses employ various strategies to overcome implementation barriers, with partnership-building emerging as a critical approach. Research by Young et al. (2013) found that successful school nurses cultivated relationships with multiple stakeholders including school administrators, teachers, parents, community health organizations, and local health departments. These partnerships provided resources, legitimacy, and support for SRH programs.

A study by Coyle et al. (2006) examining effective school-based SRH programs identified collaboration with external health organizations as particularly valuable. School nurses who partnered with family planning clinics, youth health centers, and public health departments could provide more comprehensive services through referral networks and shared resources. The research emphasized that formal memoranda of understanding and regular communication mechanisms strengthened these partnerships.

### 4.2 Advocacy and Policy Engagement

Effective school nurses engage in advocacy at multiple levels to create supportive environments for SRH implementation. Research by Lineberry and Ickes (2015) found that school nurses who actively participated in policy discussions, provided evidence-based recommendations to administrators, and educated school boards about adolescent health needs were more successful in securing support for comprehensive SRH programs.

A study by Constantine et al. (2015) highlighted the importance of data-driven advocacy. School nurses who systematically collected and presented data on student health needs, service utilization, and program outcomes were better positioned to advocate for resources and policy changes. The research found that presenting local data was particularly persuasive in demonstrating the relevance and necessity of SRH programs to specific school communities.

### 4.3 Adapting Programs to Local Contexts

Cultural adaptation and contextualization of SRH programs represent key strategies for overcoming resistance and improving effectiveness. Research by Kirby and Laris (2009) found that while evidence-based programs provide valuable frameworks, adaptation to local cultural, religious, and community contexts is essential for successful implementation. School nurses who engaged community members in program design, incorporated local values and examples, and respected cultural sensitivities while maintaining core evidence-based components achieved better acceptance and outcomes.

A study by Michielsen et al. (2010) examining SRH program adaptation in diverse settings found that successful school nurses employed a "core plus" approach—maintaining essential evidence-based components while adding culturally relevant content and delivery methods. This strategy allowed programs to remain effective while being acceptable to local communities.

### 4.4 Continuous Professional Development and Training

School nurses who prioritize ongoing professional development are better equipped to implement effective SRH programs. Research by Rushton et al. (2020) found that participation in specialized training on adolescent sexual health, counseling techniques, and cultural competence significantly improved school nurses' confidence and effectiveness in SRH service delivery.

A study by Ketting et al. (2016) identified several effective professional development strategies including: participation in workshops and conferences, peer learning networks, online training modules, clinical supervision, and mentorship programs. The research emphasized that one-time training is insufficient; rather, continuous learning and skill development are necessary to maintain competence in the evolving field of adolescent sexual health.

### 4.5 Creating Safe and Accessible Spaces

Physical and psychological safety are prerequisites for effective SRH service delivery. Research by Nystrom et al. (2018) found that school nurses who prioritized creating private, confidential consultation spaces significantly increased student utilization of SRH services. Strategies included scheduling dedicated SRH consultation times, ensuring visual and auditory privacy, and establishing clear protocols for maintaining confidentiality.

A study by Ambresin et al. (2013) examining youth-friendly health services identified several characteristics that school nurses can implement: convenient hours (including before/after school availability), minimal wait times, non-judgmental attitudes, peer education programs, and visible assurances of confidentiality. The research found that even small environmental modifications, such as posting confidentiality policies and providing anonymous question boxes, improved accessibility.

### 4.6 Utilizing Technology and Innovation

Innovative use of technology represents an emerging strategy for enhancing SRH program implementation. Research by Guse et al. (2012) found that school nurses who incorporated digital platforms—including educational websites, text messaging services, and social media—reached more students and provided more accessible information. Technology-based approaches were particularly effective for addressing stigma, as students could access information privately.

A study by Wadham et al. (2019) examining digital health interventions for adolescent sexual health found that school nurses who used technology as a complement to, rather than replacement for, face-to-face services achieved the best outcomes. Effective strategies included using videos and interactive modules for education, providing text-based appointment reminders and follow-up, and offering online platforms for anonymous questions.

### 4.7 Engaging Parents and Families

Rather than viewing parental involvement as solely a barrier, effective school nurses strategically engage parents as partners in adolescent sexual health. Research by Guilamo-Ramos et al. (2012) found that school nurses who provided parent education sessions, shared age-appropriate information about SRH curricula, and offered resources for parent-child communication about sexuality reduced parental opposition and increased program support.

A study by Akers et al. (2011) examining parent-based interventions found that school nurses who framed SRH education as supporting parental values (such as delaying sexual activity, promoting healthy relationships, and preventing harm) rather than contradicting them achieved better parental acceptance. The research emphasized the importance of transparent communication and opportunities for parental input while maintaining evidence-based program components.

## 5. Insights and Perspectives of School Nurses in Public Schools

### 5.1 Professional Identity and Role Perception

School nurses' insights regarding their professional identity significantly influence SRH program implementation. A phenomenological study by Maughan et al. (2018) explored how school nurses perceive their role in sexual health education and found diverse perspectives ranging from viewing SRH as central to their professional mission to considering it peripheral to their primary responsibilities. Nurses who integrated SRH into their core professional identity demonstrated greater commitment to comprehensive implementation despite obstacles.

Research by Willgerodt (2018) examining school nursing practice found that role ambiguity and lack of professional recognition created challenges for school nurses. Many participants expressed frustration that their expertise in adolescent health, including sexual health, was underutilized or undervalued by school administrators and teachers. The study revealed that school nurses desired greater autonomy and recognition as health professionals rather than being viewed merely as first aid providers.

### 5.2 Ethical Considerations and Moral Distress

School nurses frequently encounter ethical dilemmas in SRH implementation that provide important insights into practice challenges. A study by Goldfarb and Lieberman (2021) found that school nurses experienced moral distress when institutional policies conflicted with their professional obligation to provide comprehensive, evidence-based health information. Participants described situations where they were prohibited from discussing contraception, required to promote abstinence-only messages, or prevented from making referrals to family planning services.

Research by Bergh et al. (2015) examining ethical dimensions of school nursing practice found that nurses employed various ethical reasoning frameworks when navigating SRH dilemmas. Some prioritized parental rights and community values, while others emphasized adolescent autonomy and rights to health information. The study revealed that lack of clear ethical guidelines and institutional support for ethical decision-making left many school nurses feeling isolated and uncertain when facing complex situations.

### 5.3 Rewards and Satisfactions of SRH Work

Despite challenges, school nurses report significant professional satisfaction from SRH work. A qualitative study by Clausson and Berg (2008) found that school nurses derived deep meaning from supporting adolescents through vulnerable periods, preventing negative health outcomes, and empowering young people with knowledge and skills for healthy decision-making. Participants described moments when students returned to thank them for information or support as among the most rewarding aspects of their careers.

Research by Borawski et al. (2015) identified several sources of professional satisfaction for school nurses engaged in SRH work: witnessing positive behavior changes, building trusting relationships with students, contributing to community health improvement, and utilizing their specialized knowledge and skills. The study found that these intrinsic rewards often sustained school nurses' commitment to SRH implementation even in the absence of external recognition or support.

### 5.4 Recommendations for Policy and Practice Improvement

School nurses offer valuable insights regarding necessary improvements to support effective SRH implementation. A study by Breuner et al. (2016) synthesized school nurses' recommendations including: clearer scope of practice guidelines, mandatory comprehensive SRH training in nursing education, adequate time allocation for health education and counseling, administrative support for evidence-based programs, and protection from political interference in professional practice.

Research by Constantine et al. (2015) found that school nurses emphasized the need for standardized protocols, adequate resources, and supportive policies at district and state levels. Participants recommended establishing school nurse-to-student ratios that allow sufficient time for health promotion activities, providing dedicated funding for SRH programs, and creating professional networks for peer support and knowledge sharing.

### 5.5 Impact of Personal Values and Beliefs

School nurses' personal values and beliefs influence their approach to SRH implementation, providing important insights into practice variations. A study by Rushton et al. (2020) found that school nurses' personal religious beliefs, cultural backgrounds, and experiences with sexuality education shaped their comfort levels and approaches to SRH topics. While most participants expressed commitment to professional standards, some acknowledged that personal discomfort with certain topics (such as homosexuality or abortion) affected their practice.

Research by Pound et al. (2017) examining the influence of personal values found that effective school nurses engaged in reflective practice to recognize how their own beliefs might impact service delivery. The study identified strategies for managing value conflicts including: seeking supervision, focusing on harm reduction and student welfare, providing factual information while acknowledging diverse perspectives, and making appropriate referrals when personal limitations prevented comprehensive care.

### 5.6 Vision for Future Practice

School nurses articulate important visions for the future of SRH implementation in schools. A study by Baltag et al. (2017) found that school nurses envision comprehensive school health services that integrate SRH into holistic adolescent health care, including mental health, substance abuse prevention, and chronic disease management. Participants emphasized the importance of viewing sexual health not as an isolated topic but as interconnected with overall adolescent development and wellbeing.

Research by Denford et al. (2017) exploring school nurses' perspectives on ideal SRH programs found that participants envisioned: universal access to confidential, youth-friendly services; integration of SRH education throughout the curriculum rather than isolated lessons; strong partnerships between schools, families, and community health resources; and recognition of school nurses as essential members of educational teams. The study revealed that school nurses possess valuable insights regarding systemic changes needed to support adolescent sexual health.

### 5.7 Resilience and Coping Strategies

School nurses demonstrate remarkable resilience in continuing SRH work despite significant challenges. A study by Willgerodt (2018) examined coping strategies employed by school nurses facing opposition or barriers to SRH implementation. Participants described strategies including: focusing on individual student impact rather than systemic change, finding creative ways to provide information within constraints, building supportive peer networks, maintaining professional boundaries to prevent burnout, and engaging in advocacy efforts to create long-term change.

Research by Maughan et al. (2018) found that school nurses who maintained resilience shared several characteristics: strong professional identity, commitment to evidence-based practice, ability to navigate political and cultural sensitivities, and capacity to find meaning in small victories. The study emphasized that institutional support, including recognition of school nurses' expertise and protection from undue political pressure, significantly enhanced resilience and sustained commitment to SRH work.

## Synthesis and Gaps in the Literature

The reviewed literature demonstrates that school nurses play a critical role in adolescent sexual and reproductive health program implementation, yet face substantial challenges that impact the extent and quality of service delivery. While research from developed countries provides valuable insights into effective practices, significant gaps exist regarding SRH implementation in specific contexts such as the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) in the Philippines.

Several gaps in the literature warrant attention:

1. **Limited research in Muslim-majority contexts**: Most existing research on school-based SRH programs originates from Western or predominantly Christian contexts. There is insufficient understanding of how Islamic values, cultural practices, and community structures in regions like BARMM influence SRH program implementation and school nurses' experiences.

2. **Lack of qualitative exploration of nurse experiences**: While quantitative studies document implementation rates and barriers, fewer qualitative studies deeply explore school nurses' lived experiences, particularly their emotional responses, ethical reasoning processes, and meaning-making regarding SRH work in challenging contexts.

3. **Insufficient attention to conflict-affected and post-conflict settings**: BARMM's history of conflict and ongoing peace-building processes creates unique contextual factors that may influence health service delivery, yet research on SRH implementation in such settings is limited.

4. **Limited focus on strategies for success in restrictive environments**: While barriers are well-documented, less research examines how school nurses successfully navigate restrictive policy environments, cultural opposition, and resource constraints to provide effective SRH services.

5. **Inadequate exploration of intersectionality**: Research rarely examines how multiple identities (gender, religion, ethnicity, socioeconomic status) intersect to shape both school nurses' experiences and their interactions with diverse student populations.

This study aims to address these gaps by exploring the experiences of school nurses implementing SRH programs in public schools in SGA BARMM, providing valuable insights into practice in an underrepresented context.

## Theoretical Framework

This study is grounded in several theoretical perspectives that inform understanding of school nurses' experiences in SRH program implementation:

### Peplau's Theory of Interpersonal Relations

Hildegard Peplau's Theory of Interpersonal Relations (1991) provides a framework for understanding nurse-student interactions in SRH contexts. The theory emphasizes that nursing is a therapeutic interpersonal process involving phases of relationship development. In the context of SRH implementation, school nurses must establish trust, identify student needs, facilitate access to resources, and support adolescents in developing health-promoting behaviors. This theory highlights the centrality of the nurse-student relationship in effective SRH service delivery.

### Ecological Systems Theory

Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory (1979) offers a framework for understanding the multiple levels of influence on SRH program implementation. School nurses operate within nested systems including the microsystem (direct interactions with students), mesosystem (relationships between school, family, and community), exosystem (school policies and district guidelines), macrosystem (cultural values and national policies), and chronosystem (historical and temporal contexts). This theory helps explain how factors at multiple levels create both barriers and facilitators for SRH implementation.

### Implementation Science Framework

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) developed by Damschroder et al. (2009) provides a comprehensive framework for understanding factors that influence implementation effectiveness. The framework encompasses five domains: intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and implementation process. This framework guides exploration of how program features, community context, school environment, school nurse characteristics, and implementation strategies interact to shape SRH program delivery.

---

## Conclusion

The literature reveals that school nurses occupy a unique and vital position in adolescent sexual and reproductive health promotion, yet face complex challenges in implementing comprehensive SRH programs. Effective implementation requires not only individual nurse competencies but also supportive policies, adequate resources, community acceptance, and recognition of school nurses as essential health professionals. School nurses demonstrate remarkable resilience and creativity in navigating barriers, employing diverse strategies to provide evidence-based SRH services despite obstacles.

Understanding school nurses' experiences in specific contexts, particularly underrepresented settings such as BARMM, is essential for developing contextually appropriate interventions and policies that support effective SRH program implementation. This study contributes to filling critical gaps in the literature by exploring the lived experiences of school nurses in a Muslim-majority, post-conflict region, providing insights that can inform practice, policy, and future research in similar contexts globally.
